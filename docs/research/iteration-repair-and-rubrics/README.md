# Iteration Repair & Rubric Refinement: What To Do When X Was Missed

## The concept

AI builds the prototype. You vibe-code it a few rounds. You test it, and something was missed — the empty state, the error path, the thing that breaks at 375px — so it needs a patch. This stream studies that exact loop, **generate → iterate → test → find a miss → patch**, and the half of it nobody has built: **how the miss becomes something durable so its whole class stops recurring.**

Two joined questions:

1. **The repair.** Given a found miss, what is the mechanically best way to fix it — patch in place, regenerate the unit, roll back and re-anchor, or change the spec so the next generation is right — and what stops the fix from breaking what already worked.
2. **The ratchet.** Given a fixed miss, what should become durable, at what altitude (hook, schema, lint rule, test assertion, judged criterion, human criterion), how the criterion is written so two raters agree, and when it retires.

The short answer to the second question is the stream's most useful finding: **most misses should become gates, not rubric lines.** Four of twelve evidenced miss classes earn a judged criterion; the rest are a gate backlog. And the answer to "how are folks handling it now" is uncomfortable — they are handling it by restarting the session, and **no published measurement exists that any of the durable-record practices reduce recurrence at all.**

## Research brief: what we found

**The misses are not random, and the pattern predicts the fix.** The generator stops when the work *looks* done, so the miss set is precisely the set of properties invisible to looking: non-happy-path states, interaction wiring, edge-case data, persistence across a refresh, cross-screen flow, destructive actions without a confirm. Interaction2Code's ten named failure types put six of ten in wiring rather than missing states; WebGen-Bench, which drives generated sites with an agent instead of reading their code, puts best-model functional accuracy at 27.8%. The consequence is that "test more" is not advice: **every class except visual craft and copy has a gate that costs under an hour to build and runs in seconds**, and the honest answer to "why did this reach me" is almost always that the cheap gate was not running.

**Iteration adds misses as well as removing them, and the conversation is the part that degrades.** Roughly one applied patch in five breaks something that previously worked — and that is with a test suite present to notice, which a prototype does not have. Multi-turn performance falls about 39% against single-turn, decomposing into a ~15% loss of aptitude and a ~112% rise in *unreliability*; 40–73% of multi-turn programming tasks lose previously-correct behaviour; scope overreach accounts for 67% of agent-misalignment episodes in a 20,574-session corpus. The vendor's own remedy is amnesia rather than persuasion — after two failed corrections, clear and re-prompt — which is also the strongest argument in the stream for a durable external artifact: **the transcript is the least durable object in the loop, so the rubric, the failing test and the defect record are bets on getting state out of it.** No public measurement compares fixing forward against clearing and re-anchoring on the same defects; that experiment is a weekend.

**Most misses should become gates, and a sentence about a defect class can be worth less than nothing.** Four of twelve classes are worth a judged criterion. A defect a grep, a lint rule, an axe run or a DOM query can decide must never consume judge attention — writing it as a rubric line converts a zero-false-positive check into a probabilistic one, forever. Two findings push this further than the usual "prefer determinism": a study of six AI UI-generation tools reports that **explicitly asking for accessibility in the prompt *decreased* WCAG compliance** (relayed, unverified — hardening it is the stream's top verification priority), and rubric-trained checkpoints win 85.8% under rubric judges while losing 78.4% under rubric-free ones. So the ordering rule is asymmetric: a gate is free once built, an instruction can be negative-valued, and the rubric is only the residue after determinism has taken everything it can take. The promotion threshold for that residue is the vendor's two-strike rule, and **fix-and-forget is the correct default, recorded as a decision so the count exists next time.**

**Decomposition helps machines and hurts experts, and the craft corpus is polishing the wrong half.** Binary checklists raise LLM-judge agreement by 0.45 and lift human inter-annotator agreement too — but in medicine, experienced clinicians scored *significantly worse* than novices on binary checklists while scoring better on global ratings, and pooled inter-item reliability is 0.92 for global scales against 0.66 for checklists. A checklist measures thoroughness, and expertise shows up as omission; applied to UI it rewards the screen with the extra tooltip and penalises the restrained one. The resolution is a split, not a compromise: **binary and atomic for regression — did this known failure recur — and pairwise or holistic for quality.** Meanwhile the practitioner anti-slop corpus (43 rules, 58 gates, 7 cardinal sins, all counted from source) is almost entirely visual, while the only expert evaluation of 138 AI-generated screens against Nielsen's ten heuristics found the failures concentrated in **help, error recovery, efficiency and error prevention** — four rules in the entire corpus point at recovery, and nothing anywhere addresses help, undo, keyboard shortcuts or progressive disclosure.

**Nobody has measured whether any of this works, and nobody is selling it.** Two documents established independently that **no published measurement exists, in any venue, that a rules file or a review agent reduces recurrence of a class of miss**, and that a GitHub search for tooling that turns corrections into proposed rules returns **zero repositories**. There is no design-defect recall benchmark for any review agent, no design rubric library in any harness, no rules-file conflict linter, and no product that joins the artifact, the defect class, the fix altitude and whether it recurred. The community has argued for a year about where a miss should land without resolving it. What that costs is precise: the stream's own premise is well-motivated and unvalidated, which is why the synthesis ends in an E0–E7 roadmap whose first step is one integer per miss across ten prototypes — cheap enough to run this week and capable of ending the programme.

## Open threads

- **Measure the recurrence base rate.** One class label per miss across ten prototypes. If miss classes do not repeat across independent artifacts, no rubric can ratchet and the whole programme should stop. Nothing else should be built first.
- **Measure whether the ratchet works at all.** The repeat-offence rate — what share of this week's misses were in classes that already had a criterion — is a direct test of the stream's premise, costs one fraction a week, and is, as far as this research found, unpublished anywhere.
- **Compare fix-forward against cleared-and-re-anchored** on the same twenty defects, graded blind. The most repeated advice in the practitioner world has no measurement behind it.
- **Measure `k`, the rubric bloat parameter**, for ~$8 with a canary criterion — and settle whether the 24 / 60 / 200 rules of thumb are three findings or one model at three values.
- **Run the Hodges experiment on design artifacts:** 20 screens, 6 raters, a binary checklist and a global scale. Does the checklist invert the expert ranking, as it did in medicine?
- **Measure design-defect recall** for the screenshot loop, the browser agent and the review bots by seeding known misses. It is cheap, it does not exist, and every recommendation about which gate to build assumes an answer.
- **Harden the unverified load-bearing claims** — W4A's "prompting for accessibility made it worse" first, then Hodges/Ilgen, then CHI 2026's heuristic support rates — and re-do doc 05 from an unblocked session, reading replies rather than thread openings.
